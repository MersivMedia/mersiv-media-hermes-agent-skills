# Cinematography vocabulary (controlled terms)

Use these exact terms. In video-editing mode, camera terms DESCRIBE the source; never invent moves.


## shot_size

- Close-Up: Frames a face or another subject tightly to emphasize expression or detail.
- Dirty Single: Single subject with an edge of another character's shoulder/body intruding in foreground.
- Extreme Close-Up: Isolates a small detail, such as an eye, fingertip, or part of an object.
- Extreme Wide Shot: The environment dominates, with the subject appearing very small or distant.
- Full Shot: Shows a person’s entire body, from head to feet.
- Insert Shot: Isolated frame of a specific inanimate storytelling object.
- Medium Close-Up: Typically frames a person around the chest up.
- Medium Shot: Typically frames a person around the waist up, showing expression and upper-body movement.
- Medium Wide: Medium-wide framing is usually around the knees up. A cowboy shot is a related variation, commonly around mid-thigh up.
- Medium Wide Shot: Cuts mid thigh (originally designed in Westerns to keep gun holsters in frame).
- Over the Shoulder: Foreground shoulder/head framing character talking in the background.
- Point of View: Camera replaces character's eyes.
- Single: One character alone in the frame cleanly.
- Three Shot: Three characters grouped in frame.
- Two Shot: Two characters sharing the frame equally.
- Wide: Shows the subject within substantial surrounding space, establishing their relationship to the location.
- Wide Shot: Full subject visible head to toe with safety headroom.

## camera_angle

- Bird's Eye: Positions the camera high above the subject, typically looking steeply or directly downward.
- Chest Level: Positions the camera at approximately the subject’s chest height.
- Dutch Angle: Rolls the camera sideways, making the horizon and normally vertical lines appear slanted.
- Eye Level: Positions the camera at approximately the subject’s eye height, usually looking straight toward them.
- Ground Level: Positions the camera close to the floor or ground.
- High Angle: Positions the camera above the subject looking downward.
- Hip Level: Positions the camera at approximately the subject’s hip height.
- Knee Level: Positions the camera at approximately the subject’s knee height.
- Low Angle: Positions the camera below the subject looking upward.
- Overhead: Looks down from above, typically directly or nearly directly downward. Bird’s-eye can also describe a broader elevated view.
- Shoulder Level: Positions the camera at approximately the subject’s shoulder height.
- Top Down: Height: 90 degree downward view over a flat surface or workspace.
- Worm's Eye: Height: Placed directly on ground level looking steeply upward.
- Worm’s-Eye View: Looks steeply upward from very near ground level.

## camera_movement

- Aerial: Records from an airborne platform, such as a drone or helicopter. Aerial describes the viewpoint, not one movement.
- Arc: Moves around a subject along a curved or circular path.
- Camera Roll: Rotates the camera around its viewing axis during the shot, making the image turn.
- Crane: Uses a supporting arm to move the camera through space, often vertically or in sweeping arcs.
- Crane Shot: Movement: Camera mounted on large sweeping mechanical arm.
- Dolly In: Moves the camera physically toward or away from the subject, conventionally using a dolly.
- Dolly Out: Movement: Camera physically glides backward away from subject.
- Dolly Zoom: Combines camera travel with an opposing zoom, keeping the subject roughly the same size while background relationships change.
- Gimbal: Uses a pivoting support to stabilize camera orientation. Modern handheld camera gimbals commonly use motors.
- Handheld: Allows natural movement from a camera physically operated by a person without complete stabilization.
- Lateral Tracking: Moves the camera alongside a subject as they travel through space.
- Leading Tracking Shot: Travels ahead of a moving subject while looking back toward them.
- Pan: Rotates the camera left or right from a fixed position.
- Pedestal Up: Raises or lowers the camera itself, rather than tilting it.
- Pull Out: Movement: Smooth retreat away from subject.
- Push In: Movement: Subtle, creeping physical advance toward subject.
- Rack Focus: Movement: Lens focus ring pulls between foreground plane and background plane.
- Slow Pull-Out: Gradually moves the camera away from the subject.
- Slow Push-In: Gradually moves the camera toward the subject.
- Snorricam: Mounts the camera to a performer’s body, often facing them, so they stay relatively fixed in frame as the surroundings move.
- Static: Keeps the camera’s position and orientation fixed during the shot.
- Static Frame: Keeps the camera completely still while action unfolds within the composition.
- Steadicam: Uses a branded stabilization system, traditionally with a vest, sprung arm, and balanced sled, to isolate much of the operator’s movement.
- Tilt: Rotates the camera upward or downward from a fixed position.
- Tracking: Travels with a subject or through a space. Tracking is a broad term, not a specific support system.
- Tracking Shot: Movement: Camera travels alongside, in front of, or behind moving subject.
- Truck Left: Moves the camera sideways rather than rotating it.
- Whip Pan: Movement: Ultra fast pan creating heavy motion blur.
- Whip Tilt: A very fast tilt, usually producing motion blur.
- Zoom In: Changes focal length during recording to narrow or widen the view without requiring camera travel.
- Zoom Out: Movement: Focal length widens on a stationary camera.

## framing

- Asymmetry: Uses unequal arrangements on different sides of the frame, with or without visual balance.
- Centered Composition: Places the main subject or point of interest near the center of the frame.
- Clean Single: Frames one character without another character intruding into the foreground.
- Diagonal Composition: Uses diagonal lines or arrangements to direct attention across the frame.
- Dirty Single: Frames one character while keeping part of another character, often a shoulder or head, visible in the foreground.
- Foreground Framing: Uses nearby elements to border, partially obscure, or add depth around a more distant subject.
- Frame Within a Frame: Uses doors, windows, architecture, foreground objects, mirrors, or other shapes to create a secondary frame around the subject.
- Group Shot: Features a group together, usually more than three people.
- Headroom: The space between the top of a person’s head and the upper edge of the frame.
- Layered Composition: Arranges meaningful elements across foreground, middle ground, and background.
- Leading Lines: Uses visible lines to guide attention toward or through important parts of the image.
- Look Room: Look room is space in the direction a subject looks; lead room is space in the direction they move.
- Negative Space: Uses relatively empty areas around a subject as an active part of the composition.
- Over-the-Shoulder: Frames a subject past another person’s shoulder or head, keeping that person partly visible in the foreground.
- Point of View: Shows a view intended to represent what a character sees.
- Profile Framing: Shows a subject from the side, usually emphasizing the face’s side outline.
- Reflection Framing: Uses a mirror, glass, water, or another reflective surface to show or arrange subjects.
- Rule of Thirds: Places important elements near the lines or intersections of a three-by-three grid.
- Symmetrical: Organizes visual elements around the center of the frame with strong balance or symmetry.
- Symmetry: Arranges corresponding shapes or subjects on opposite sides of an axis.
- Three-Shot: Features three people together within one frame.
- Two-Shot: Features two people together within one frame.

## lens

- 100mm: Telephoto: isolates a smaller part of the scene.
- 135mm: Telephoto: tighter framing from farther away.
- 14mm: Ultra-wide: captures a very broad view.
- 18mm: Ultra-wide: includes extensive surroundings.
- 200mm: Telephoto: a narrow view for distant subjects or details.
- 21mm: Wide-angle: slightly tighter than 18mm.
- 24mm: Wide-angle: useful for subjects with substantial context.
- 28mm: Wide-angle: tighter than 24mm.
- 32mm: Moderately wide: between 28mm and 35mm.
- 35mm: Moderately wide: includes a subject and nearby surroundings.
- 40mm: Near-normal: between typical wide and standard views.
- 50mm: Standard: a middle-range field of view on full frame.
- 65mm: Longer than standard: tighter framing than 50mm.
- 75mm: Short telephoto: a narrower view for subject emphasis.
- 85mm: Short telephoto: useful for tighter framing at greater distance.
- Deep Focus: Keeps foreground, middle ground, and background elements relatively sharp at the same time.
- Rack Focus: Changes focus from one subject or depth plane to another during the shot.
- Shallow Focus: Keeps a narrow plane of the image sharp while allowing foreground or background elements to fall out of focus.
- Wide-Lens Close-Up: Uses a relatively wide lens positioned close to the subject.

## focus

- Deep Focus: Keeps subjects across a substantial depth range, often foreground through background, acceptably sharp.
- Deliberate Defocus: Intentionally moves the subject or scene out of focus for an expressive effect or transition.
- Focus Pulling: Adjusts focus during a shot to maintain sharpness on a moving subject or redirect attention.
- Hyperfocal Focusing: Sets focus so acceptable sharpness extends from approximately half that distance to infinity, under the chosen sharpness criterion.
- Lens Tilt: Tilts the lens relative to the sensor to change the orientation of the plane of focus.
- Rack Focus: Shifts focus between subjects or details at different distances during one continuous shot.
- Shallow Depth of Field: Keeps a narrow range of distances acceptably sharp while nearer or farther areas blur.
- Soft Focus: Deliberately reduces fine-detail contrast for a softer image, using suitable optics, diffusion, or processing.
- Split Diopter: Uses a partial close-up attachment to focus on a near subject in one area and a farther subject elsewhere simultaneously.
- Zone Focusing: Sets focus and aperture to cover a chosen distance range, then keeps the subject within that range.
